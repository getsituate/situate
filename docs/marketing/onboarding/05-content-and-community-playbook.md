# 5. Content and community playbook

Content can help people recognize organizational problems, understand Situate's intended benefit and share what matters in their routines. The topic areas and formats below provide a starting point for content planning. Channel priorities, publishing cadence and targets will develop through the initial content experiments.

## The work behind the task

Explore the gathering, checking and coordination hidden behind a simple action. A short reply may require three sources; planning a small event may involve several changing details.

A short video or carousel could show the information someone needs before answering one question. Responses may reveal which parts of this work people repeat most often.

## Keeping related information together

The birthday story on [reboot.getsituate.com/#story](https://reboot.getsituate.com/#story) follows a venue's request for guest numbers, dietary requirements, a running order and sound equipment. The answers are spread across notes, a PDF and earlier email.

The scenes depict a Gmail draft with the running order attached and a calendar entry for the key handover. These actions are simulated; use the supplied previews with an illustrative-preview label. The story shows why related information needs to stay connected.

## Tools that fit the person

Explore differences in working habits: tasks in project notes, separate lists or a personal reading collection. Show how someone could ask for a view suited to their needs and refine it through conversation.

A comparison of two approaches can invite people to explain when each is useful and what they currently adapt by hand.

## Preparing work for review

Show what the system prepares, what the person reviews and where their judgment matters. An annotated draft can make the relevant context and unresolved questions visible.

Explore what would make the review worthwhile and which details people would still want to check themselves.

## Channels to explore

TikTok, Instagram, YouTube Shorts and X are candidate channels. Prioritize them according to audience fit, the content idea and the time available.

# Try a content idea

## A flexible experiment brief

For a content experiment, record the audience situation, the question being tested, the creative choice and the response that would inform the next decision. Match the level of detail to the size of the experiment.

- **Audience situation:** who would recognize the problem, and where?
- **Question:** what do we want to learn?
- **Hypothesis:** what response do we expect, and why?
- **Creative choice:** which hook, example or format are we testing?
- **Evidence:** what response would support or challenge the hypothesis?
- **Next decision:** what would we change or investigate afterward?

Example hypothesis: “People who keep tasks inside project notes will recognize duplicate-list maintenance and describe their workaround.”

## Read the response

When useful, vary one important element, such as the hook. Treat small organic comparisons as directional: audience, timing and distribution can affect the result.

**Attention:** reach, views and watch time show whether content was seen and held attention. **Interest:** relevant replies, visits or sign-ups reveal questions and possible next steps. **Adoption:** actual use and return visits help assess product fit. Platform counts may use different definitions. Likes or sign-ups alone do not establish sustained use or willingness to pay.

## Birthday-reply example

**Situation:** the website's birthday story: a venue reply prepared from notes, a PDF and earlier email.

**Hook A:** “The details are spread across three places.”

**Hook B:** “One reply. Three places to look first.”

**Body:** show how the sources provide context for a reply with the running order attached. Use the supplied previews with a visible illustrative-preview label.

**Conversation prompt:** “What information do you usually have to gather before you can send a simple reply?”

Review the relevance and detail of responses alongside reach and views. Genuine product recordings must reflect the actual experience shown. Use only confirmed availability, integration and release information in the ending.

# Community conversations

When someone describes a problem, ask about a recent instance: “When did this last happen, and how did you deal with it?” For example: “The last time you needed information from several places to answer an email, where did you look?” Follow the details that matter to the person.

Do not request private documents, account details, credentials or correspondence in public comments. Do not quote or redistribute sensitive material someone posts.

## Reply with care

- **A familiar problem:** acknowledge the difficulty; follow up when useful.
- **A product question:** verify availability, prices, privacy and release dates before answering. Say when an answer is still unconfirmed.
- **A bug or failed attempt:** ask for the goal and observed behavior. Do not assure someone that no data changed or that an external action failed unless that is known.
- **Criticism:** respond to the experience described, with room for disagreement and no pressure to try the product.

## Learning notes

Record the situation, the problem, the workaround, the impact and the open question. Keep observations and interpretations clearly identified.

Use reviewed, non-identifying summaries in shared content documents. Removing a name alone may leave a story recognizable. Keep private messages in an appropriate restricted location; do not turn them into testimonials or enter them into an AI tool without an appropriate basis.

## Feedback from people who have tried Situate

Use these questions as a menu. A useful conversation may cover just one or two.

- What did you want help with?
- How clear were the introduction and first steps?
- What did you try, and what happened?
- Where did you get stuck, and how did that affect the task?
- What helped, or what one change would make the experience more useful?

For ongoing feedback, one observation about the goal, expectation, experience and impact is enough; technical diagnosis is optional.

Identify whether the person used the separate prototype, the main application or only saw an illustrative preview. Note the build or device when it affects interpretation.
