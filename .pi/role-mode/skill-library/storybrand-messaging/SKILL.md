# StoryBrand messaging

Make the customer the protagonist and the product a useful guide. Use Donald Miller's seven-part framework to clarify a message, not to force every advertisement into seven sections. The framework is a communication heuristic, not evidence of product value or a conversion guarantee.

## Establish the brief and evidence

Identify the audience, their situation, one desired outcome, the offer, the channel, and the next action. Read existing research, positioning, and the actual destination before drafting. Ask only questions whose answers materially change the message; label remaining assumptions in working notes.

For Situate, read repository-relative `docs/positioning.md` and `design/brand/docs/voice.md`. Read `design/brand/README.md` before visual direction. Check current product or release evidence for each capability mentioned. Approved requirements and workshop demonstrations do not establish availability. Do not silently replace approved positioning or broaden product scope.

Keep a compact claim ledger when claims need verification: proposed claim, supporting source, qualification, and whether it can be published. Missing evidence means removing or qualifying the claim, not inventing a testimonial, statistic, guarantee, integration, or release. Keep private research out of public artifacts.

## Clarify the seven elements

Draft a short internal message map. Use plain language and one coherent customer situation.

| Element         | Question                                             | Constraint                                                                                                                              |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Character       | Who wants what concrete outcome?                     | Choose one primary desire for this message, not a list of every feature.                                                                |
| Problem         | What prevents that outcome?                          | Separate the external obstacle, internal frustration, and underlying fairness or value question. Treat inferred feelings as hypotheses. |
| Guide           | Why trust this product to help?                      | Pair understanding of the situation with relevant, verifiable competence. Do not make the company the hero.                             |
| Plan            | How does the person get started and reach value?     | Show a few accurate steps, often three. Do not conceal setup, costs, permissions, or prerequisites to make the plan look easy.          |
| Action          | What should the person do next?                      | Choose a clear primary action that matches an available destination. An optional lower-commitment action must be genuinely useful.      |
| Avoided failure | What realistic cost persists if the problem remains? | Use proportionate stakes. No shame, invented urgency, catastrophe, or manufactured anxiety.                                             |
| Success         | What becomes concretely better?                      | Describe an observable outcome without promising an unsupported transformation.                                                         |

The philosophical layer need not appear in final copy. Do not manufacture a moral conflict for an ordinary inconvenience. Likewise, an ad can communicate value without explicitly describing failure.

If relevant, name the change in how the person can act or see themselves. Ground it in the task rather than making grand claims about who they will become.

## Turn the map into a message

1. Write a plain-language sentence connecting the customer's problem, the offered help, and a concrete outcome. Treat this as a diagnostic, not a mandatory sentence template.
2. Draft a benefit-led headline, a supporting explanation of the offer, relevant proof, and a clear action. Readers should understand what is offered without having to decode a slogan.
3. Translate features into useful consequences while preserving the mechanism and limits. Avoid vague substitutes such as "effortless," "intelligent," or "everything in one place."
4. If alternatives are requested, offer two or three genuinely different angles, such as the immediate problem, desired outcome, or demonstrated mechanism. Keep the offer and evidence constant so comparison is meaningful.
5. Recommend an angle for its fit to the audience and channel, not because it sounds more impressive. Distinguish editorial judgment from tested performance.

Keep message maps, evidence notes, and rationale separate from publishable copy. Do not expose labels such as "hero," "guide," or "SB7" in an advertisement unless the advertisement is about the framework itself.

## Make the message visible

Choose the smallest presentation that makes the claim understandable:

- **Advertisement:** one main idea, a visual that explains or demonstrates it, and a legible action. Do not rely on tiny disclaimers to correct a misleading headline.
- **Landing page:** make the offer, benefit, and next action apparent early. Add the problem, demonstration, plan, proof, objections, and repeated action only where they answer reader questions. Do not impose seven sections or hide the offer behind a long story.
- **Product demonstration or promotional storyboard:** show the starting difficulty, the relevant action, and the result. Separate actual product behavior from proposed or simulated behavior in accompanying notes. Do not depict unavailable capabilities as shipping.

For a visual handoff, specify each beat's reader question, visible copy, visual evidence, intended takeaway, and next action. Give text and imagery distinct jobs instead of illustrating generic enthusiasm. Preserve readability, contrast, mobile composition, and a static or reduced-motion route to the same information. Never require watching an animation to discover the offer or act.

Use canonical brand assets. A character or mascot can guide attention but should not displace the customer's problem and outcome.

## Review and deliver

Check whether a reader unfamiliar with the brief can answer:

- What is this, who is it for, and what problem does it address?
- What would improve, and why should I believe it?
- What happens after I take the next step, including relevant costs or commitments?

Check every claim against its source and every action against its destination. Remove fabricated social proof, absolute privacy claims, hidden conditions, pressure tactics, and unsupported comparisons. Preserve uncertainty and user choice.

If testing is requested, ask representative readers to explain the offer and expected next step in their own words before asking whether they like it. Record misunderstandings separately from preferences. For conversion experiments, define the intended action and downstream quality measure; clicks alone do not demonstrate comprehension or customer value. Never report an imagined walkthrough as user research.

Deliver the requested scope: copy, critique, message map, or visual handoff. For a new campaign, a compact default is the audience and outcome, recommended headline and supporting text, action and destination, visual direction, and unresolved evidence needs. Do not create a full campaign, modify product strategy, or publish content without authorization.
